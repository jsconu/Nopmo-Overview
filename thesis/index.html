Product thesis

Ondatrace isn't another project management tool.

Teams already have tools to track their work. What's missing is a way to see across them.

The first signs that a project is in trouble are usually scattered: an overdue Jira ticket, a customer email, something said in a meeting, a calendar clash, and a status update written three days later. Ondatrace brings those together so someone can act on them.

Six bets we're testing

  1. 1

    Start from what happened, not from a question

    A question typed into a chatbot is gone once it's answered. An update from a project tool has a source, a time, an owner and a project. Keeping those details means Ondatrace can treat an update the same way whichever tool it came from, and keep a history.

  2. 2

    Let clear rules decide, and use AI to explain

    Many health checks come down to simple facts: a date moved, a dependency isn't done, a blocker is still open, nobody owns a task, a budget passed its limit.

    When a rule can decide, the rule decides. The AI explains the result and handles the cases rules can't.

  3. 3

    Remember what came before

    If you only see today's status, you can't tell whether a problem is new, getting worse, getting better or already fixed. Ondatrace keeps that history, because the trend matters as much as the current state.

  4. 4

    End with a decision or next step

    Summaries help, but managers need to know what needs attention, who has to decide, what's blocked and what the options are.

    Ondatrace tracks decisions, dependencies, risks and suggested actions as records of their own, so they don't get lost inside a status paragraph.

  5. 5

    Earn trust one action at a time

    How much Ondatrace should do on its own depends on what's at stake. From low stakes to high:

    1. Refreshing data from a tool
    2. Drafting an internal update
    3. Editing a task
    4. Moving a committed milestone
    5. Sending a message to a customer

    Ondatrace takes on one kind of action at a time, within fixed limits, keeping a record of what it did. A person can always step in.

  6. 6

    Work out the structure before asking for it

    Real work is messy. The same project goes by different names in email, chat, meetings, tickets and spreadsheets. Dates conflict. Dependencies come up in meetings and never reach the tracker. Some important work has no project record at all.

    Ondatrace connects to your tools as they are, suggests the projects and links it thinks exist, and asks people to confirm or fix them. You don't have to build a tidy setup first.

    How it should work

    1. Read your tools
    2. Suggest the structure
    3. Show the evidence
    4. You confirm or fix it
    5. Keep watching

    Not this

    1. Set everything up
    2. Keep it updated by hand
    3. Ask AI about it

    The test we use

    Could Ondatrace find this out, or make a reasonable guess, before asking someone to type it in?

    People's time should go to judgment calls, corrections, decisions and approvals. Not data entry.

What would prove us wrong

We'd count Ondatrace as a failure if the people using it find that:

  • Seeing across tools doesn't tell them much beyond what their tools already show.
  • Its suggestions are too noisy to be worth a manager's time.
  • The evidence is there but too hard to check.
  • Keeping its records in order takes more effort than it saves.
  • Its review list becomes another inbox nobody trusts.
  • It can't explain why it did something, or why it didn't.
  • Fixing and supervising it takes more time than it saves.

The prototype is built to answer these questions.
